# Pixel 9a on AT&T - a second unit, and the USB ceiling in plain view

**This is a different physical phone from the three Google Fi Pixel 9a records**,
not the same handset with another SIM. Same make and model, but a distinct USB
serial and a distinct tether MAC (`enx2e9319548995` here against
`enxce1d58e89c0f` there). That distinction is the whole value of this record: it
gives a second, independent sample of the same model.

Host TCP config is unchanged from every record since
[2026-09-05-pixel-9a-bbr](2026-09-05-pixel-9a-bbr.md) - BBR,
`slow_start_after_idle=0`, `mtu_probing=1`.

## Setup

Came up on its own, DHCPed by the same generic `Wired connection 1` profile,
dual-stacked, sole default route. The razr was unplugged first.

Bound to `cdc_ncm`, and the NTB parameters are identical to the other Pixel:
`rx_max`/`tx_max` 16384, matching the device-advertised `dwNtbInMaxSize` /
`dwNtbOutMaxSize` exactly. The "raise these to 32768" advice remains a no-op on
this model, now confirmed on two separate units.

The IPv6 prefix is 2600:381::/32, which is AT&T - consistent with the SIM, and
a different network from the 2607:fb90::/32 T-Mobile space behind Google Fi in
the other records.

## Observations

**Aggregate hit 273 Mbps, the highest in the log**, against 246 for the Google Fi
Pixel and 234 for the razr. On a 480 Mbps bus that is roughly 91% of the
realistic USB 2.0 bulk ceiling (~300 Mbps). By the README's rubric this is no
longer a hint - it is the USB 2.0 ceiling in plain view, and it is a cable or
port problem rather than anything tunable in software.

**Single-stream was extraordinarily consistent: 106 / 100 / 104 Mbps, a 1.06x
spread.** That is the tightest in the log by a clear margin - the razr managed
1.34x and the best Pixel BBR pass 1.4x. Three transfers landing within 6 Mbps of
each other is not a congestion-control problem by any reading.

**But the shape does not match any of the README's three diagnostic cases**, and
it is worth naming. The rubric covers wide-spread-with-flat-RTT (congestion
control collapsing) and single-approximately-equal-to-aggregate (WAN-limited).
Here the spread is nearly nil *and* aggregate is 2.6x the best single. A single
flow reproducibly stops at ~104 Mbps while four together reach 273, with no
variance to suggest anything is collapsing. That points at a **per-flow** limit -
carrier shaping per connection, or a single flow simply unable to fill the
bandwidth-delay product - rather than at the path, the host, or congestion
control. A fourth rubric case may be warranted if this recurs.

RTT averaged 36.8 ms with 7.0 ms mdev, the highest mdev recorded here. The
spread is misleading though: the first ping returned 50.0 ms and the remaining
four settled to 30-33 ms. That ping ran immediately after the four-stream test,
so the outlier is most likely residual queue draining rather than a genuinely
jittery path.

Zero errors and zero drops across roughly 44k RX / 11k TX packets.

## Issues

Nothing had to be done to bring it up. Two problems surfaced afterwards, when
`adb` was installed and this phone authorised.

### The radio type is contradicted, and is now unresolved

`carrier.network` was written as `5G` on the operator's report at test time.
Reading the device afterwards gives:

    gsm.network.type    LTE,Unknown        (dual-slot; the populated slot is LTE)

The field has been **blanked rather than flipped to LTE**, because neither
reading establishes what the radio was doing during the transfers:

- The adb reading is post-hoc. The radio may genuinely have changed between the
  pass and the reading - that is ordinary behaviour, not a fault.
- But the report of 5G came from reading the handset, and **AT&T brands
  LTE-Advanced as "5G E" in the status bar**. A status-bar "5G" on this carrier
  specifically is not evidence of 5G NR. The razr and the Flip6, both on Google
  Fi, reported `NR_SA` unambiguously over adb; nothing here ever did.

The README defines this field as the radio actually in use at test time, so an
honest blank is correct. Resolving it needs `gsm.network.type` read *during* a
pass, which is now what the skill instructs.

**This matters beyond one field.** A Fi-vs-AT&T comparison drawn from these
records treated AT&T as the 5G leg and the unrecorded Fi radio as the confound.
If AT&T was in fact on LTE, that reasoning inverts. No comparison across these
records should assume a radio that was never recorded.

### The USB product ID differs from the one under test

With USB debugging on, the phone enumerates as `18d1:4eec`, not the `18d1:4eeb`
recorded above - a composite configuration adding ADB, exactly as the razr moved
`2e24` -> `2e25`. **`usb.product_id` has deliberately not been updated**: `4eeb`
is what was on the bus during the transfers, and that is what the record
describes. The same applies to the BOS/SuperSpeed reading, which was taken
before adb existed on this host.

### Post-test metadata

    ro.build.id                     CP41.260814.003.B1
    ro.build.version.security_patch 2026-08-05
    ro.product.model                Pixel 9a

`os` is **Android 17**, and the adb device name is `tegu_beta` - this handset is

before attributing any behaviour here to the Pixel 9a as a model: the network
stack on a beta OS is not necessarily the shipping one. `carrier.name` AT&T is
confirmed. No measurement changed.

## Follow-ups

**The cable is now the single most valuable thing to test in this repo.** The
evidence has narrowed considerably:

    Pixel 9a (Google Fi):  advertises SuperSpeed, enumerates at 480
    Pixel 9a (AT&T, this): advertises SuperSpeed, enumerates at 480
    razr 2024:             advertises no SuperSpeed, enumerates at 480

Two independent SuperSpeed-capable phones, on two different carriers, both
pinned at 480 Mbps, with aggregate now at 91% of what that bus can carry. The
phone is ruled out on both counts. What is **not** ruled out, and what this
record cannot distinguish, is whether the cable or the host port is responsible -
it is not known whether the same cable was used across these three runs, and no
device on this host has ever trained SuperSpeed. Testing a known-good USB 3
cable, on a port whose SuperSpeed companion bus is confirmed idle, would settle
it in one pass.

Also outstanding:

- **Re-run this pass with the radio type captured during it.** The 5G/LTE
  contradiction above cannot be settled retrospectively, and it undercuts any
  carrier comparison drawn from this record.
- The per-flow ceiling at ~104 Mbps. Worth re-running at another time of day to
  see whether the figure is stable enough to be a real shaping limit.
